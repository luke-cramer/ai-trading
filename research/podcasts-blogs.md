## 1. Podcasts

### Chat With Traders (chatwithtraders.com, since 2015)
General finding: despite branding as trader interviews, guests who discuss *systematic* approaches skew toward people who went full-time/professional, or who are outright fund managers — genuinely "still solo, still part-time, still retail-scale" guests with published numbers are rare. This itself is a data point: the podcast circuit selects for survivors and escalators, not steady-state hobbyists.

- **Ep 152 — Kory Hoang, "A beginner's foray into (part-time) systematic trading"** [anon] (https://www.traderammo.com/stocks/chat-with-traders-episode-152/, https://www.investingshortcuts.com/traders-podcast/12-1-17-kory-hoang-interview-chat-traders-episode-152/). Ex-Pitchbook private equity analyst running "numerous," deliberately simple algorithmic strategies across ETFs, ETNs, and some crypto, developed while employed full-time. Description characterizes results only as "decent gains on his trading capital" — no percentage returns, drawdown, or capital size surfaced in show notes. He later quit his job to trade + run startups full-time, which is the survivorship-bias flag: the podcast highlights the guy who scaled up, not the larger population who ran similar "simple ETF/ETN algo" books and got nothing worth a podcast slot.
- **Ep 281 (rebroadcast of Confessions of a Market Maker Ep 57) — Marsten Parker** [verified, via Schwager's *Unknown Market Wizards*] (https://chatwithtraders.com/episode/281-marsten-parker-the-purely-systematic-wizard-trader, https://mhptrading.com/about.html). Described as the only fully systematic trader in *Unknown Market Wizards*. Ex-software engineer, went full-time in 1998, 100% mechanical strategies (breakouts, mean reversion, IPO plays). Claimed **>20% annualized return over 22 years with only 2 losing years**, vs. S&P's 5.7% annualized over the comparable 20-year window. Built his own institutional-grade backtester (RealTest) as a side product. **Read with heavy survivorship discount**: he is in a "Market Wizards" book *because* he is a multi-sigma outlier among people who tried this; the book's entire premise selects for the tiny tail of systematic retail traders who didn't blow up or plateau over 20+ years. No cost/fee breakdown or capital-size figure surfaced.
- **Ep 225 — Kyle Schultz (Ravinia Investment Management)** [verified-ish, registered CTA] — explicitly *not* a solo retail case; he runs a registered commodity trading advisor. Included here only to note that the podcast's "systematic trading" tag conflates registered fund managers with actual retail hobbyists — filter guest lists carefully if reusing this podcast as a source.

**Bottom line for CWT**: no guest in this sweep disclosed a clean "here is my P&L, capital, and costs as a still-solo retail trader" data point. Anecdotal advice extractable: keep strategies simple, diversify instruments, expect years not months to know if an edge is real.

### Better System Trader (bettersystemtrader.com, host Andrew Swanscott, 12+ yrs own trading US/AU equities)
This is the most directly on-point podcast for the brief — Swanscott himself is a working systematic retail trader, and the show explicitly targets "systematic traders of all levels," not fund managers.

- **Ep 201 — Kris Longmore, "Getting serious about part-time trading"** [promo-adjacent — Longmore sells Robot Wealth education, see §2, but this specific content is his general risk framework, not a pitch] (https://bettersystemtrader.com/201-getting-serious-about-part-time-trading-kris-longmore/). Concrete, quotable claims:
  - **"A Sharpe ratio of 1 to 1.5 [is] a realistic long-term target for a part-time retail trader."** This number recurs across his podcast appearance and blog (see §2) and is the single most load-bearing "what's achievable" data point in this whole sweep.
  - Backtest-scenario framing: even at a "true" 1.0 Sharpe, one simulated path shows a 1.5 realized Sharpe with a 117-day drawdown, another shows 0.5 realized with a **~2-year drawdown** — same underlying edge, wildly different lived experience. Direct relevance to "babysitting time": a statistically fine part-time system can still deliver two straight years that feel like failure.
  - Three behaviors he says "almost guarantee failure": **trading too frequently** (cost drag), **sizing too large** (variance drag on compounding), and **"trying to be a hero"** with low-probability speculative bets.
  - **"Backtesting is not research"** — warns of the "backtest cycle of doom," i.e., iterating rules against the same data until they're overfit, dressed up as validation.
- Other flagship guests (Larry Williams Ep 020, Linda Raschke Ep 049, Van Tharp Ep 024 "Why Most Traders are Unsuccessful," Tom Basso) are legacy discretionary/market-wizard figures, not solo-retail-systematic case studies with disclosed numbers — useful for psychology/risk framing, not for "what's the realistic net-of-cost edge" question.
- **Rob Hanna** (VIX vs SPX episode) [promo] runs Quantifiable Edges, a paid research subscription — treat any specific edge claims from that episode as advertising for the subscription rather than disclosed personal results.
- **Laurens Bensdorp** (Ep "Confident Consistent Trading") [promo] — sells a systematic-trading course/book ("Automated Stock Trading Systems" education business); repeat pattern of this podcast circuit: the "solo retail systematic trader" guests who *do* have polished numbers to show usually monetize via a course, so the clean track record and the sales pitch arrive in the same breath.

### Flirting with Models (host Corey Hoffstein, CIO of Newfound Research — institutional systematic asset manager)
Also institutionally-framed by default, but explicitly features occasional independent/solo traders — directly useful here.

- **Scott Phillips, "Finding Ugly Edges in Crypto Markets" (S7E15)** [anon-ish; independent trader, not selling a product in this episode] (https://www.flirtingwithmodels.com/episodes/fr15zGd7rV7). Described as "the second independent trader interviewed for the show." Key finding, near-verbatim from the search synthesis: **independent/solo traders find that capital pool size, execution efficiency, and lack of supporting infrastructure make anything other than "loose-pants trend following" difficult in traditional (equity/futures) markets** — i.e., at true solo-retail scale, sophisticated mean-reversion/stat-arb/market-making strategies in traditional markets are largely infrastructure-gated, not idea-gated. His response was to move into **crypto perpetuals**, where he trades trend-following plus structural features specific to that market: **cost-of-carry/funding-rate dynamics on perps**, volatility normalization/targeting, and what the episode summary calls "crime indicators and price-insensitive trading patterns" (i.e., forced flows from hacks, liquidations, and non-economic actors) as exploitable, retail-accessible edges. Dedicated "advice for new traders" segment exists (~1:05:10 mark) but wasn't recoverable in text form via fetch — full audio/transcript worth pulling directly if this thread gets prioritized.
  - **Direct relevance to the user's brief**: this is the clearest practitioner statement that *venue choice* is itself a capital-efficiency decision — crypto perps let a solo trader access edges (funding harvesting, liquidation-flow exploitation) that would need institutional execution infrastructure to access in equities/futures.

## 2. Blogs

### Robot Wealth (Kris Longmore, robotwealth.com)
[promo — sells the "Trade Like a Quant" bootcamp, $447 one-time with 7-day refund, run twice yearly, claimed 1,000+ retail students taught (https://robotwealth.com/trade-like-a-quant-bootcamp/); discount specific performance framing accordingly, but the *methodology* content is consistent with his non-monetized podcast appearances above, which cross-validates it]
- Core teaching: **risk-premium harvesting** via liquid, diversified ETFs with explicit volatility management, plus **"uncompetitive inefficiencies"** — his term for mechanical, calendar-driven flows too small for institutions to bother with (e.g., month-end bond-fund window dressing in TLT, equity/bond relative-performance reversals). This is a specific, actionable retail-edge category: hunt flows institutions are structurally too big to harvest.
- Also teaches ML-assisted equity pairs/universe selection — but frames this as harder and less durable than the ETF risk-premium sleeve.
- Course materials **explicitly disclaim performance**: "No guarantees (nothing in trading comes with those)." Founder's own anecdote of turning $10k → $25k in four months is explicitly labeled **luck, not skill or a replicable system** by Longmore himself — notable candor from someone whose business model is selling the education. This self-report is one of the more credible data points in the whole sweep precisely because it argues against the seller's own interest.
- Recurring quantitative anchor (also stated on Better System Trader, §1): **Sharpe 1.0–1.5 is the realistic long-run target for a part-time retail systematic trader.** Treat this as the sweep's best single consensus number for "what should a solo dev with a day job expect."
- Failure triad again: overtrading, oversizing, hero trades.

### Ernie Chan (epchan.blogspot.com; also runs QTS Capital Management and PredictNow.ai)
[promo — active seller of a paid workshop/course circuit ("two-day deep dive… AI-driven trading strategies," with Imperial College MathSoc) and of PredictNow.ai's ML-risk-management SaaS/subscription; discount return/edge claims accordingly, but decay/regime framing below is standard academic-quant consensus, not a sales point]
- **Alpha decay has two distinct causes** he separates explicitly: (1) **competition/crowding** — too many participants running the same strategy compresses the edge; (2) **regime shift** — macro or market-structure change invalidates the original mechanism entirely, independent of crowding. Practical implication for a solo builder: post-mortem a dead strategy by asking which failure mode it was, because the fix differs (crowding → find a less-followed niche; regime shift → the strategy's causal premise no longer holds, don't just re-optimize parameters).
- **"Causal strategies… are still superior, more robust, and easier to tweak if they should begin to decay"** — i.e., prefer strategies with an explainable *why* (a mechanism, a forced flow, a structural constraint) over pure statistical pattern-mining, because when a causal strategy decays you can diagnose and adapt it; a data-mined black box just breaks.
- Advises diversifying **across strategy types** (merging long/short-vol, trend/mean-reversion) rather than diversifying only across instruments within one strategy type — crowding and regime risk are correlated within a strategy family.
- 2025 output is heavily GenAI/LLM-pivoted (co-authored *Generative AI for Trading and Asset Management*, May 2025) — signals where the "quant thought leader" content market is heading, but treat specific LLM-alpha claims from this circuit as tied to product promotion (PredictNow.ai, the workshop) until independently backtested.

### QuantStart (Michael Halls-Moore, quantstart.com)
[promo — sells *Successful Algorithmic Trading* and related books/mentorship via Gumroad; the blunt tone below cuts somewhat against the sales incentive, which adds a little credibility]
- Headline framing, repeated across his material: **"Algo trading is NOT a get-rich-quick scheme – if anything it can be a become-poor-quick scheme."**
- Standard-but-correct risk-first framing: evaluate a strategy's risk attributes (drawdown, leverage, tail behavior) before its headline return — consistent with the Longmore/Chan material above.
- Positions itself for people with "basic programming skills" wanting to build "fully automated" systems in Python — i.e., squarely the user's own profile (dev-first, cost-of-build near zero). No disclosed personal live P&L found in this pass; content is instructional/product-oriented rather than a practitioner track record.

### Alpha Architect (alphaarchitect.com)
[promo-adjacent — Alpha Architect is a registered investment adviser that runs its own factor ETFs, so blog content indirectly markets a fund lineup even though individual posts are academic/quant-research in tone, not sales copy]
- **April 2025 post on theoretical vs. tradable factors**: real-world implementation costs — **trading fees and shorting expenses reduce factor-strategy returns by an estimated 2–4% per year** versus the theoretical (frictionless) factor return. This is a concrete, citable "cost tax" number for anyone building a factor/smart-beta-style systematic sleeve at retail scale; shorting cost in particular is an underweighted line item for solo builders who backtest long/short factors on frictionless data.
- General posture: research-forward, well-sourced (links underlying academic papers), useful for *why* a factor should work, but doesn't itself disclose a solo-retail live track record.

### Quantocracy (quantocracy.com — aggregator, no independent voice, surfaces others)
Useful as a discovery layer; notable 2025 pieces it surfaced (https://quantocracy.com/recent-quant-links-from-quantocracy-as-of-05312025/, https://quantocracy.com/category/best-of/):
- **Harvey, Mazzoleni & Melone, "The Unintended Consequences of 164 Profitable Trading Strategies"** [verified — 1st place, Quantpedia Awards 2025] — academic audit of a large strategy universe; title alone signals the sweep's recurring theme: published "profitable" strategies routinely have hidden costs, capacity limits, or crowding effects that erode the headline number. Worth pulling in full for the decay-focused beat of this research spike if not already covered elsewhere.
- **"I Asked 6 LLMs for Better Exit Strategies" (Rogue Quant)** [anon/practitioner blog] and **"Can We Finally Use ChatGPT as a Quantitative Analyst?" (Quantpedia)** [verified-ish, research shop] — both indicate 2025's retail-facing quant blogosphere is actively probing LLMs for signal generation/analysis rather than full autonomous trading, consistent with the caution seen elsewhere in this sweep (see §3).

### Financial Hacker (financial-hacker.com — Zorro platform-affiliated author)
[promo-adjacent — author has a commercial interest in the Zorro trading engine/scripting language the posts are built around, but the specific quantitative conclusions below argue *against* short-term retail algo trading, i.e., against the easy sell, which raises credibility]
- **Hardest, most directly actionable cost-math finding in the whole sweep** (https://financial-hacker.com/is-scalping-irrational/): given typical retail FX costs (0.5 pip spread + $0.60 commission per 10k units), breakeven win rate is **~90% on 1-minute bars vs. ~53% on 1-day bars**. Author: *"This exceeds the best performances of real trading systems by a large amount."* Personal admission: **"I never managed to develop a strategy with a significantly positive walk-forward test on bar periods less than 30 minutes."** Direct implication for the user's project: **do not build sub-hourly/scalping systems at retail cost structures — the math doesn't clear no matter how good the signal is**; daily bars (or slower) is where retail algo edges have to live.
- **EUR/CHF grid-trading blowup, author's own capital** (https://financial-hacker.com/build-better-strategies/) [anon but specific, first-person]: a grid strategy exploiting the SNB's 2011–2015 EUR/CHF floor produced a backtested **130% annual return, Sharpe 1.7** while the floor held — then blew up when the SNB removed the floor in January 2015 without warning. Framed explicitly as a cautionary tale: **"simple systems based on very obvious inefficiencies can be extremely profitable, but have a limited lifetime"** — the strategy's edge was a specific policy-enforced price ceiling, not a durable market inefficiency, and it died the instant the policy did. This is the sweep's cleanest concrete "how edges actually die" story with numbers attached, and it's a first-person account of the author's own loss, not a hypothetical.
- Separate long-term-portfolio post ("Get Rich Slowly," https://financial-hacker.com/get-rich-slowly/): monthly-rebalanced multi-asset risk-parity-style system, ~12.9% annualized in an **in-sample** backtest (discount accordingly — no out-of-sample or live number given), 1:4 leverage; author found **shorter (daily/weekly) rebalancing showed no advantage and reduced profit through added trading costs** — another data point for "trade less often" at retail scale. Also flags that Markowitz-style mean-variance portfolios have historically failed live without explicit weight constraints, because the optimizer concentrates into unstable corners.

### Only VIX (onlyvix.blogspot.com)
Coverage here is mostly VIX/VXX/UVXY mechanics rather than a disclosed personal track record: contango/backwardation dynamics, and roll-cost decay in leveraged/inverse vol ETNs making them structurally unsuitable for anything but short holding periods. The strongest practitioner-relevant warning distilled from this space: **naive trend-following logic applied directly to VIX gets destroyed by the product's own mean-reversion and roll-yield mechanics** before a system has time to prove itself — i.e., VIX/vol products are a "know the instrument's plumbing first" trap for retail builders porting a generic trend-following framework onto a new asset.

## 3. LLMs in trading, 2024–2026 (cross-source)

Retail-facing commentary here splits sharply into (a) institutional/academic signal on what's technically possible, and (b) low-quality SEO/affiliate content dressed as practitioner experience. Separate them explicitly:

**Higher-credibility signal:**
- **Man Group's "AlphaGPT"** [verified — disclosed by a $151B systematic hedge fund] — GPT-4 fine-tuned on historical internal research notes to generate 50–100 strategy ideas/day, paired with a code-generation agent that turns strategy descriptions into executable Python. Directionally useful as "what the frontier looks like," but this is built on proprietary research-note training data and institutional research infrastructure retail cannot replicate — treat as an indicator of direction, not a template.
- **Academic LLM-momentum study** [verified, arXiv, semi-independent] — LLM-extracted sentiment from firm-specific news improved a cross-sectional momentum strategy's Sharpe/Sortino both in-sample and in a limited out-of-sample window *after* the model's pretraining cutoff (i.e., a genuine forward test, not just a backtest the model could have memorized). Promising but thin: single study, limited OOS window, no disclosed transaction-cost treatment in the summary available.
- **Quantpedia's "Can We Finally Use ChatGPT as a Quantitative Analyst?"** [verified-ish research shop, semi-promotional for Quantpedia's own subscription products] — measured, mixed-to-positive framing: useful as a junior-analyst-style assistant for idea triage and code scaffolding, not as an autonomous alpha source.

**Practitioner build logs (mixed credibility, genuinely retail-scale):**
- **dev.to, "Everyone Is Talking About AI Trading Bots. We Actually Built One."** [anon, but detailed and credible — a real open-source build log] (https://dev.to/crashland/everyone-is-talking-about-ai-trading-bots-we-actually-built-one-heres-what-happened-4cb3). Built a market-making bot for Polymarket (prediction markets), pivoted from a cross-venue arbitrage idea after that edge "disappeared" almost immediately. Paper-traded only (no real capital risked). Result: **719,624 orders placed, only 1,212 filled (0.17% fill rate)**, and fills skewed 8:1 into the losing side of the book (inventory risk, not a broken algorithm). Author's own conclusions, directly relevant to this user's build-cost-near-zero profile: **"the hard part is order flow management," not the trading model** — infrastructure (race conditions, order-state tracking) ate more engineering time than alpha research; **"paper trading doesn't tell you whether your strategy works"** because it can't capture market impact or how informed counterparties adapt to your quoting pattern; small market-makers face structural adverse selection — sophisticated players "offload the risk onto you" during directional moves. Net: technically solid, alpha-negative, and says so plainly — a rare honestly-reported null result.

**Low-credibility / discount-heavily bucket** — surfaced via search, could not verify methodology or independent sourcing for any of the following; treat as SEO/affiliate content until a primary source is found, not as evidence:
- Claims of **"73% of automated crypto trading accounts fail within six months"** and **"80%+ of retail bot users underperform buy-and-hold after fees"** — no traceable primary source (a follow-up search to find the underlying methodology was not completed before this session's search budget was exhausted; flag as unresolved rather than cite as fact).
- A cited **"Crypto Quant Strategy Index, July 2025"** claiming **34% ROI for AI-assisted (human-in-the-loop) trading vs. 29% for fully automated bots over six months** — no identifiable publisher or index methodology found; likely a fabricated or unaudited marketing statistic. Do not treat as a real number.
- Multiple near-identical Medium posts ("I Lost $14,000 Testing Crypto Bots," "I Tested 23 Trading Bots and Lost $14,000," "I Built a Crypto Trading Bot and Lost Real Money") — templated, listicle-adjacent structure typical of affiliate content marketing a bot vendor or course rather than reporting an actual personal loss; **[promo/unverifiable]**, exclude from any quantitative synthesis.

**Synthesis for the user's project**: 2024–2026 practitioner-grade evidence supports LLMs as a *tooling accelerant* (idea generation, code scaffolding, news/sentiment feature extraction, exit-rule brainstorming) rather than as a standalone alpha source with disclosed, audited retail-scale live returns. No credible source in this sweep reports a real-money, live, LLM-driven retail trading track record with a positive net-of-cost result over a meaningful sample period.

## 4. Cross-cutting synthesis for the user's brief

- **Realistic edge ceiling, part-time/solo, net of costs: Sharpe ≈ 1.0–1.5**, converged on independently by Robot Wealth (blog + podcast) and implicitly consistent with QuantStart's risk-first framing. Treat any pitch promising much more than this at retail scale as either survivorship bias (Marsten Parker-style outlier) or a sales pitch.
- **Trade slower, not faster.** Financial Hacker's cost math (90% win rate needed at 1-minute bars vs. 53% at daily) and the "Get Rich Slowly" monthly-vs-daily rebalancing finding both point the same direction: retail cost structures make sub-hourly/scalping systems close to mathematically unviable; daily-bar-or-slower is where a solo dev's edge has to live.
- **Prefer causal/structural edges over statistically-mined ones** (Ernie Chan's framing, Robot Wealth's "uncompetitive inefficiencies," Financial Hacker's SNB-floor story as the negative case study of what happens when the *structural* reason for an edge disappears). Know *why* a strategy should make money before automating it, so you can tell crowding-decay from regime-death when it stops working.
- **Venue/instrument choice is a capital-efficiency lever, not just a market choice** — the Flirting with Models "independent trader" finding that traditional-market sophistication is infrastructure-gated at solo scale, while crypto perps expose retail-accessible structural edges (funding/cost-of-carry, forced/liquidation flows), is probably the single most strategically useful data point for a build-cost-near-zero, capital-constrained solo operator.
- **Babysitting-time reality check**: Longmore's own simulated-path example (identical 1.0 "true" Sharpe strategy showing a ~2-year drawdown in one draw) is a direct warning that even a statistically sound part-time system can *feel* broken for a very long stretch of real time — budget for that psychologically before going live with real capital.
- **Promo ledger** (who's selling what, for discounting purposes): Robot Wealth sells a $447 bootcamp; Ernie Chan sells workshops + PredictNow.ai SaaS; QuantStart sells books/mentorship; Alpha Architect runs factor ETFs; Rob Hanna (Better System Trader guest) sells Quantifiable Edges subscriptions; Laurens Bensdorp (same podcast) sells a systematic-trading course; Financial Hacker is tied to the commercial Zorro platform. None of this invalidates their specific quantitative claims, but every "realistic return" number above should be read against the seller's incentive to make retail systematic trading look achievable.
- **Failure/decay stories actually found, with numbers**: (1) Financial Hacker's EUR/CHF grid strategy, 130% annual return / 1.7 Sharpe collapsing to a real loss the day the SNB depegged (Jan 2015) — structural-edge death; (2) dev.to's Polymarket market-maker, technically clean build, 0.17% fill rate, alpha-negative even before real capital was risked — infrastructure ≠ edge; (3) generalized crowding/capacity erosion documented academically in the Quantocracy-surfaced "164 Profitable Trading Strategies" study.
